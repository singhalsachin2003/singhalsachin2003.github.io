# Lehman Brothers, 2008

*Nine hundred thousand trades, closed out at once*

Lehman Brothers Holdings filed for bankruptcy on 15 September 2008 with about $639bn of assets, the largest bankruptcy in US history. It was counterparty to roughly 900,000 derivative transactions, and its failure was an event of default under nearly every ISDA it had signed. What followed was the largest close-out the market had ever run — bilateral counterparties valuing and terminating on their own terms, and a clearing house managing the cleared book through its default process. The two halves went very differently, and the difference reshaped the market.

## Key terms

- **Event of default** — A failure listed in the ISDA, such as bankruptcy of a party or its credit support provider, that lets the other side terminate.
- **Close-out netting** — Terminating every trade under one agreement and reducing them to a single net amount owed one way.
- **Close-out Amount** — The 2002 ISDA valuation standard: what it would cost to replace the terminated trades, determined in good faith.
- **Section 2(a)(iii)** — The ISDA condition that a party need not pay while an event of default by the other is continuing.
- **Default management** — A clearing house’s process for hedging and auctioning a failed member’s portfolio, funded first by that member’s margin.
- **Rehypothecation** — A broker reusing client assets it holds as collateral, which turns them into a claim if the broker fails.

## In practice

The legacy is the market structure most desks now work inside: standardised swaps cleared through central counterparties with rehearsed default auctions, variation and initial margin on uncleared trades, close-out valuation procedures written down before they are needed, and the ISDA resolution stay protocols that stop counterparties terminating en masse when a large bank fails.
